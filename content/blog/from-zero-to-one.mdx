Early products rarely need more possibilities. They need a smaller set of clear decisions.

When I start with a blank page, I try to replace the question “What could this become?” with a more useful one: **What is the smallest complete version of this idea?** Complete does not mean packed with features. It means the product has a point of view from beginning to end.

## Find the sentence first

Before designing screens or choosing a stack, I write one sentence that describes the change the product creates for a person. It should be concrete enough to disagree with.

> A useful product statement names the person, the problem, and the outcome—without hiding behind a feature list.

That sentence becomes a filter. If a feature does not make the promised change clearer or easier, it can wait. This is less glamorous than an expansive roadmap, but it gives every design and engineering decision a shared center.

## Design the path, not the pages

A polished collection of screens can still feel like a fragmented product. I map the shortest path from arrival to value, then make the transitions between those moments explicit.

1. What does someone understand in the first ten seconds?
2. What is the first meaningful action they can take?
3. How does the interface show that something changed?
4. What makes the next action feel obvious?

Thinking in paths exposes the quiet details: empty states, loading behavior, useful defaults, and the copy that keeps someone oriented. Those details are usually where trust is won.

## Let the first version teach you

The goal of version one is not to prove that every assumption was right. It is to make the important assumptions observable.

Build the real interaction early. Put it in front of people. Watch where their confidence rises and where it drops. Then use that evidence to decide what deserves more craft and what should disappear.

The best zero-to-one work feels decisive, but it is not rigid. It is a sequence of small, testable commitments that gradually turns uncertainty into a product.
